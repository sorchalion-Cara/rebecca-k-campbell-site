The Book

A verse-novel of the ancient Amazons

Long before history thought to set them down, there were said to be women who needed no man's permission to ride, to hunt, or to take up arms — the Amazons, spoken of by nations that could imagine such freedom only as legend. The Lay of the Vixens recovers that old rumour as verse, tracing its riders from the wide grass of the steppe to the clamour of battle, and from there into the longer, stranger passage by which the living become myth. Told in the cadence of a song handed down rather than a history written outright, it asks what survives of a woman's courage once only the story of it remains.

In Its True Colours

Riders beneath a rising moon

In Greek report, the Amazons were held to be a nation entire unto themselves — riders and archers who met even the most celebrated of heroes as equals in the field, dwelling, so it was said, at the very edge of the known world. Little of their history survives except in fragments, filtered through centuries of retelling by those who feared or admired them in equal measure. The Lay of the Vixens takes up that old, incomplete thread and answers it in song — a lyric composed for riders who refused, against every expectation, to be forgotten.
